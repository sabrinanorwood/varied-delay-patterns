


The study design was optimized for improving tolerability (RQ1) rather than improving efficacy/effectiveness (RQ2): the conditions differed in their delays in early weeks but all used 10 second delays in their final weeks. The goal was to decrease attrition in early weeks with a gradually more intensive intervention.

No significant differences in tolerability were found between conditions; estimated marginal mean dropout rates were similar between conditions (all pairwise p-values between XXX and XXX, dropout proportions ranging from XX to YY; see Figure XX). A non-preregistered dose-response analysis was run as a sensitivity analysis, also not detecting any differences (all pairwise p values > .XXX).

Differential efficacy was also assessed using two metrics: number of app open attempts (primary) and proportion of aborted open attempts (secondary). However, the design was not optimised for this: all conditions finished on 10 second delays in week 5 and 6, making differential efficacy less likely a priori. For the number of app open attempts outcome, pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences between the ACC condition and the others (pairwise p values from XX to YY), with no other significant pairwise differences (all p > XXX). No pairwise differences were found for the secondary efficacy outcome (all ps > .XXX). A non-preregistered dose-response analysis was was added as a robustness test. This also has the benefit of being an Intention To Treat (ITT) analysis whereas the preregistered one was Per Protocol (PP), which can be confounded by attrition. Results suggest that the number of app open attempts is lower when the friction delay is longer (see figure XX). 

Together, results do not provide support for the hypothesis that shorter friction delays improving tolerability, and b) either do not provide support for the hypothesis that shorter friction delays improve efficacy or, in the exploratory analyses, that the longer existing 10 second delays may provide better efficacy. 

Future studies with a) designs optimised for quantifying differential efficacy or effectiveness (i.e., differential delays sustained to the final week, delays longer than the standard 10 seconds) are needed. Future research can be improved by employing practices standard in the clinical trials literature, such as preregistered ITT analyses as standard (REFs) and employing active or passive control conditions. While robust decreases in the proportion of app opening attempts were observed (i.e., -38 to -55% between conditions CHECK), very high rates of attrition were also observed (c.70% between conditions CHECK). In the absence of an active or passive control condition, it therefore remains unclear whether friction delays are truly effective or whether observed decreases are statistical artifacts (e.g., due to attrition).
